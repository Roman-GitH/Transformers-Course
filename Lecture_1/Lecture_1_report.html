Stanford CME 295 · Transformers & Large Language Models · Lecture 1

From words to numbers to self-attention

How do you make a machine read? Cut the text into tokens, give each token a vector, let every token look directly at every other token — and you have the Transformer.

A The general idea

Models only understand numbers; text is a string of characters. Lecture 1 builds the bridge in five moves, each one fixing a weakness of the previous move:

What problem is being solved?

NLP tasks fall into three buckets (below). All of them need a representation of text that a model can compute with, that preserves meaning, and that depends on context (a bank by a river is not a bank that lends money).

The key insight in one line

Instead of reading a sentence word by word and hoping a hidden state remembers the start (RNN), give every token direct links to all other tokens and let the model learn how much to look at each: attention. “Attention is all you need” (2017).

B The exposition of the idea

B0 · First: how do we even measure an NLP model?

Classification: the four possible outcomes

Take any yes/no task: is this e-mail spam?, is this review toxic?, does this X-ray show a tumour? One of the two answers is the positive class — by convention the thing you are looking for (spam, toxic, tumour); the other is the negative class. “Positive” does not mean “good”. For every example there are two separate facts: what is actually true (the label) and what the model predicted. Two facts with two values each give four outcomes. Each name has two words: the second word is the model's prediction, the first word says whether that prediction was right.

Mnemonic: T/F = was the model right? P/N = what did the model say? The four counts add up to the number of examples $N$; $TP+FN=P$ is the number of actual positives, $FP+TN$ the number of actual negatives.

Accuracy is the share of all predictions that are right — the diagonal of the table above:

$$\text{accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}=\frac{\text{correct predictions}}{\text{all predictions}}$$

Equivalent form that explains the trap. Let $\pi=P/N$ be the share of positives (the prevalence), $\text{recall}=TP/P$ and the false-alarm rate $\text{FAR}=FP/(FP+TN)$ (share of negatives wrongly flagged). Then

$$\text{accuracy}=\pi\cdot\text{recall}+(1-\pi)\cdot(1-\text{FAR})$$

If positives are rare ($\pi\to0$) accuracy is almost entirely $1-\text{FAR}$: it only measures how well you handle the easy majority. A model that never predicts “positive” has recall 0 and FAR 0, hence accuracy $=1-\pi$ — e.g. 99 % when 1 % of the mails are spam, while being useless.

Worked example: 10 000 e-mails, 100 of them spam (π = 1 %)

Precision, recall, F1: two different questions about the positive class

$$\text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad F_1=\frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}$$
  • Precision – look only at what the model flagged (TP+FP, the left column). “When it raises the alarm, how often is it right?” In the example 70/(70+198) = 26 %: three out of four alarms are false.
  • Recall (sensitivity, hit rate) – look only at what is really positive (TP+FN, the top row). “Of all spam, how much did it find?” 70/100 = 70 %.
  • TN appears in neither: the pile of easy negatives cannot inflate them. That is why they expose what accuracy hides.
  • F1 is the harmonic mean: it is dragged towards the smaller of the two (precision 1.0 and recall 0.0 give F1 = 0, not the 0.5 an ordinary average would give), so you cannot buy a good score by maximising only one.
  • The two pull against each other: a stricter threshold means fewer false alarms (precision ↑) but more misses (recall ↓).

Generation: BLEU, ROUGE, perplexity

For generated text, BLEU and ROUGE compare the output against a reference text (expensive: someone must write the references). Perplexity needs only the model's own probabilities and measures how surprised the model is by a text: lower is better (BLEU/ROUGE: higher is better).

$$\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\Big(-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(w_i\mid w_{\lt i})\Big)$$

Reading the formula from the inside out:

  • $w_1,\dots,w_N$ is the text, split into $N$ tokens; $w_i$ is the i-th token (the one that actually appears).
  • $w_{\lt i}=(w_1,\dots,w_{i-1})$ is the prefix: everything that comes before position $i$. For $i=1$ it is empty (or just <bos>).
  • $p(w_i\mid w_{\lt i})$ is the probability the model gave to the true token $w_i$, having read only the prefix. Operationally: feed the prefix, take the model's softmax distribution over the whole vocabulary for the next position, and read off the entry belonging to the token that really came next. All other entries are ignored.
  • $\log p(\cdot)$ is the natural log: $0$ if the model was certain and right ($p=1$), $-0.69$ for $p=\tfrac12$, $-4.6$ for $p=0.01$. It is always $\le0$.
  • The minus sign makes it positive: $-\log p$ is the surprise (in nats) of token $i$. Averaging over the $N$ positions gives the average surprise per token, i.e. the cross-entropy. This is the same quantity as the training loss $-\log\hat y_{\text{next}}$ of B2, averaged over a text.
  • $\exp(\cdot)$ turns “average nats per token” into a more intuitive number (use the same base for log and exp).

Equivalently, by the chain rule $p(w_1\ldots w_N)=\prod_i p(w_i\mid w_{\lt i})$ is the probability the model assigns to the whole text, and $\mathrm{PPL}=p(w_1\ldots w_N)^{-1/N}$: the inverse geometric mean of the per-token probabilities.

itrue token $w_i$prefix $w_{\lt i}$$p(w_i\mid w_{\lt i})$ (assumed)$\log p$
1the⟨bos⟩0.5−0.693
2catthe0.25−1.386
3satthe cat0.125−2.079

Average of $\log p$ $=-4.159/3=-1.386$, so $\mathrm{PPL}=e^{1.386}=4$ (check: $(0.5\cdot0.25\cdot0.125)^{-1/3}=64^{1/3}=4$). Interpretation: the model is as uncertain as if it had to pick uniformly among 4 equally likely words at every step. Two anchors: a perfect model has $\mathrm{PPL}=1$; a model that guesses uniformly over a vocabulary of $V$ words has $\mathrm{PPL}=V$ (our mini model: $V=31$). Perplexities are comparable only for the same tokenization and vocabulary.

The accuracy trap – every number disclosed

Setup. $N$ = 10 000 examples; you set the share of positives π with the first slider, so $P=\pi N$ positives and $N-P$ negatives. “Always negative” never predicts positive, so by construction TP = FP = 0 (recall 0, false-alarm rate 0). “Detector” is a hypothetical classifier whose behaviour is fully described by two numbers you control with the other sliders. The defaults (recall 70 %, false-alarm rate 2 %) are illustrative assumptions, not measured or derived; before they were hidden constants in the code, now they are inputs. Everything else is computed from them.

B2 · Step 2 – give each token a meaningful vector

The simplest vector is one-hot: a vector with a single 1 (vocabulary size $V$ entries). To compare two token vectors the standard measure is the cosine similarity – the cosine of the angle between them:

$$\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}\in[-1,1]$$

Same direction → 1, orthogonal → 0, opposite → −1. Problem: any two different one-hot vectors are orthogonal, so teddy bear is as unrelated to soft as to book. We want similar meanings to give high cosine.

Cosine similarity in 2-D

Rotate the two vectors. The two one-hot vectors of a 2-word vocabulary sit at 0° and 90°.

Learning embeddings with a proxy task (word2vec)

Idea: don't design the vectors, learn them. Pick a proxy task whose answer we can read from raw text — predict the next word (or, in word2vec: predict a word from its neighbours = continuous bag-of-words; predict the neighbours from a word = skip-gram). A model that predicts well must have captured how language works; the hidden layer it builds is the embedding. Nobody cares about the task itself, only about the vectors it leaves behind.

Formally, for the current token with one-hot $\mathbf x$:

$$\mathbf h=\mathbf x W_1+\mathbf b_1\in\mathbb R^{D},\qquad \hat{\mathbf y}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf h W_2+\mathbf b_2)\in\mathbb R^{V},\qquad \mathcal L=-\log \hat y_{\text{next}}$$

and gradient descent (backpropagation) updates $W_1,W_2$ to make the true next word more probable. Training stops when the proxy loss stops improving (“converged”) — but the real goal is useful vectors, which depends on the downstream task. Typical $D$: hundreds to thousands (e.g. 768); a bigger $D$ is richer but makes everything after it more expensive.

Reading the loss $\mathcal L=-\log\hat y_{\text{next}}$

The network's last layer is a softmax: it turns $V$ raw scores into $V$ probabilities $\hat{\mathbf y}=(\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_V)$ that are positive and sum to 1. $\hat y_j$ is “how likely the model thinks word $j$ is to come next”. Training pairs say which word did come next; call its index next. Then

  • $\hat y_{\text{next}}$ is simply the probability the model gave to the correct word (one entry of the vector; the other $V-1$ entries are ignored by the loss, although softmax couples them: raising one lowers the rest).
  • $-\log\hat y_{\text{next}}$ is the surprise at the truth. It is the general cross-entropy $-\sum_j y_j\log\hat y_j$ with the target $\mathbf y$ being one-hot ($y_{\text{next}}=1$, all other $y_j=0$): every term but one vanishes.
  • Averaged over all training pairs this is the quantity gradient descent minimises; $\exp$ of it is the perplexity of B0.
probability given to the true word $\hat y_{\text{next}}$10.50.11/31 (uniform guess)0.01→ 0

Why this shape? Being confidently wrong is punished much harder than being unsure. Why is it convenient? With softmax the gradient of the loss with respect to the raw scores is just $\hat{\mathbf y}-\mathbf y$ (predicted minus target): the true word's score is pushed up in proportion to how far its probability is from 1, every other word's score down in proportion to its probability. The hand-written backpropagation in toolkit.py starts from exactly that line (dZ = (probs - T) / N). A numeric example from the trained mini model is in the results dashboard (chapter E).

word2vec in a bit more detail

The idea behind all of this is the distributional hypothesis: a word is characterised by the company it keeps (Firth, 1957). Words used in similar contexts (cute, fluffy, soft) end up with similar vectors, and raw text provides the training labels for free (self-supervision). word2vec (Mikolov et al., Google, 2013) made this fast enough to train on billions of words, in two variants. Example sentence “a cute teddy bear is”, window of 2 words on each side:

Our mini model uses the simplest possible version of the same recipe: input = current word, target = the next word (a window of one, to the right). It is the one-hidden-layer ancestor of a language model (compare Bengio et al., 2003). The network has two matrices: $W_1$ ($V\times D$, one “input” vector per word) and $W_2$ ($D\times V$, one “output” vector per word). word2vec practice is to keep $W_1$ (some people average the two).

Beyond the lecture (1) A softmax over $V=100\,000$ words at every training step is expensive, so word2vec replaces it by negative sampling: instead of “which of $V$ words comes next?” it asks a yes/no question, “is this (word, neighbour) pair real or did I draw the neighbour at random?”, using the true pair plus ~5–20 random wrong ones. The embeddings that come out are similar. (2) The learned space has a surprising linear structure: $\text{vec}(\text{king})-\text{vec}(\text{man})+\text{vec}(\text{woman})$ lands near $\text{vec}(\text{queen})$ – approximately and for hand-picked examples, but it shows that directions in the space can encode relations. (3) Related methods: GloVe (2014, factorises co-occurrence counts), fastText (2016, adds subword pieces, so unseen words get vectors).

“Dropping” the proxy task

Nobody wants a next-word predictor from this network; we want the by-product. After training we throw away $W_2$, the softmax and the loss – they were scaffolding – and keep only the lookup table $W_1$: token $i$ ↦ row $i$. The task is called a proxy because it stands in for the real goal “vectors that are useful for later tasks (sentiment, search, translation…)”. That is why a low proxy loss is only a means: we judge embeddings by how they behave afterwards (neighbours make sense, same-topic cosine high, downstream accuracy), not by the loss alone. The same pattern – pretrain on a free task, reuse the representation – recurs throughout the course (masked-word prediction in BERT, next-token prediction in GPT). In modern LLMs there is no separate step any more: the embedding table is one more set of weights inside the big network, trained jointly with all layers on next-token prediction, and the “proxy” is the model's own training objective.

B3 · Step 3 – make vectors depend on context: RNNs and why they fail

A recurrent neural network (RNN, 1980s) reads tokens one at a time and carries a hidden state $\mathbf h_t$ (also called activation or context vector) – a summary of the sentence so far:

$$\mathbf h_t=\tanh\!\big(W\mathbf h_{t-1}+U\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b\big)$$

Order now matters, and the last hidden state can feed a classifier (sentiment) or a decoder (translation). LSTM (1990s) adds a second track, the cell state $\mathbf c_t$, to keep what is “important to remember”. Three limitations remain:

  1. Long-range dependencies / vanishing gradient. The meaning of the whole sentence is squeezed into one hidden state. Backpropagating from the last word to word $t$ multiplies one Jacobian per step: $\partial\mathbf h_T/\partial\mathbf h_t=\prod_{k=t+1}^{T}\partial\mathbf h_k/\partial\mathbf h_{k-1}$. A product of many numbers below 1 goes to 0 (vanishing); above 1 it blows up (exploding).
  2. Slow. To get $\mathbf h_T$ you must compute all earlier states – no parallelism across positions.
  3. It's a bottleneck: everything must pass through one vector.

What is ρ, and how does it relate to the Jacobian?

Yes – ρ is a knob on the Jacobian of the chain rule above. In the simple RNN, $\mathbf h_k=\tanh(\mathbf z_k)$ with $\mathbf z_k=W\mathbf h_{k-1}+U\mathbf x_k+\mathbf b$. The Jacobian of one step (the matrix of all partial derivatives $\partial h_{k,a}/\partial h_{k-1,b}$) is, by the chain rule,

$$J_k=\frac{\partial\mathbf h_k}{\partial\mathbf h_{k-1}}=\underbrace{\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1-\mathbf h_k^{2}\big)}_{D_k:\ \tanh'\text{ at each unit, entries in }(0,1]}\;\cdot\;W$$

and the sensitivity of the last state to a state $m$ steps back is the product of $m$ such matrices:

$$\frac{\partial\mathbf h_T}{\partial\mathbf h_{T-m}}=J_T\,J_{T-1}\cdots J_{T-m+1},\qquad \Big\lVert\frac{\partial\mathbf h_T}{\partial\mathbf h_{T-m}}\Big\rVert\;\le\;\prod_{k}\lVert D_k\rVert\,\lVert W\rVert\;\le\;\rho^{\,m}\quad(\text{if }\lVert W\rVert=\rho).$$
  • The norm used is the spectral norm of a matrix: the largest factor by which it can stretch a vector. $\lVert\partial\mathbf h_T/\partial\mathbf h_{T-m}\rVert$ answers: “if I nudge the state $m$ steps ago by a unit, how much can the final state move?” That is exactly the “gradient norm” on the plot's vertical axis, and the “distance in tokens” on the horizontal axis is $m$.
  • ρ is the scale of the recurrent matrix: in torch_model.gradient_decay $W=\rho\,Q$ with $Q$ a random orthogonal matrix (a rotation/reflection: it preserves length). So every singular value of $W$ equals ρ: $W$ shrinks every vector by exactly ρ if ρ<1, stretches it by ρ if ρ>1, keeps its length if ρ=1. In a real RNN $W$ is not so tidy, and ρ stands for “typical size of its largest singular value”.
  • The bound $\rho^m$ is the grey dashed line. It ignores $D_k$ (set to 1); the real factors $D_k$ are ≤ 1 (equal 1 only when a unit is exactly 0), so the measured curves lie below the dashed line. This is why ρ=1.0 still decays, and why ρ=1.2 does not explode in the plot: tanh saturation clamps it. With no saturating non-linearity, ρ>1 would give $\rho^m$ growth: exploding gradients (cured in practice by gradient clipping).
  • Backpropagation through time multiplies the loss gradient by exactly these Jacobians, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf h_t=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf h_T\cdot J_T\cdots J_{t+1}$, so a word $m$ steps back contributes a learning signal of size ≲ $\rho^m$ – effectively nothing for $m=20$, ρ=0.8 ($0.8^{20}\approx0.01$, and the measured value is far smaller).

Take-away: with the RNN, “what happened 20 words ago” has an influence of ≈10⁻⁶ or less on the gradient; through attention the influence stays of order 10⁻²–1 whatever the distance – there is a path of length 1.

B4 · Step 4 – attention: look directly at what you need

Attention (2014) first appeared in translation: when producing the next French word, the decoder takes a peek at the relevant part of the input instead of relying on one hidden state. The Transformer (2017, Attention Is All You Need) drops the recurrence entirely: self-attention computes the representation of every token as a function of all the tokens, via direct links, in parallel.

First, what exactly gets embedded? From “a vector per word” to “a vector per word in its context”

After B2 every token type owns one row of an embedding table: wherever bank appears, the lookup returns the identical vector. Self-attention keeps the table but adds a second stage that rebuilds each token's vector from its neighbours:

$$\underbrace{\mathbf x_i}_{\text{lookup (same every time)}}\ \longrightarrow\ \underbrace{\mathbf z_i=\sum_{j}a_{ij}\,\mathbf v_j}_{\text{a blend of all tokens' vectors, weights } a_{ij}\text{ depend on this sentence}}$$

Tiny illustration (invented 2-D numbers). Let the stored vectors be $\mathbf v_{\text{bank}}=(1,0)$, $\mathbf v_{\text{river}}=(0,1)$, $\mathbf v_{\text{cash}}=(0,-1)$, and suppose “bank” gives weight ½ to itself and ½ to its clue word:

sentenceinput vector of banknew vector $\mathbf z_{\text{bank}}$
“the river bank”(1, 0)½·(1,0) + ½·(0,1) = (0.5, +0.5)
“robbing a cash bank”(1, 0)½·(1,0) + ½·(0,−1) = (0.5, −0.5)

Same input, different outputs, because the weights $a_{ij}$ differ. So you are right that something bigger than a single token is encoded: the output at a position is a vector for “this token as used in this sentence”, a summary of the token plus the part of its context that matters for it. Consequently a word no longer corresponds to one point in the embedding space but to a cloud of points, one per occurrence; the shape of the cloud is the word's range of senses.

How to visualise it. Run many sentences containing bank through the network, collect the output vector at the bank position each time ($d$ numbers), project them to 2-D with PCA (or t-SNE / UMAP in practice), and draw one dot per occurrence, coloured by the sense. A static embedding would give a single dot; a contextual one gives two separated clusters (river vs money). The “bank” widget further down is exactly this plot, from a small trained Transformer; it also marks the dot of the sentence you select. (In deep models this contextualisation builds up layer by layer: probing studies find that upper layers separate word senses best.)

Query, key, value

Think of a soft dictionary lookup. A Python dict compares your query with each key, finds the one exact match and returns its value. Attention does the same but softly: it compares the query with every key, turns the match scores into weights that sum to 1, and returns the weighted blend of all the values. To make this learnable, each token's embedding row $\mathbf x$ (a row of $X$) plays three roles and gets a separate learned projection for each (the sizes $n,d,d_k,d_v$ are defined in the box below the figure):

$$Q=XW_Q,\qquad K=XW_K,\qquad V=XW_V$$
  • query – “what am I looking for?” (e.g. for teddy bear);
  • key – “what do I offer?”: compared with the query to decide how similar they are;
  • value – “what I give you if you attend to me”: the vector that is actually averaged.

The comparison is a dot product, normalised into weights by a softmax, which then weight the values:

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)V\;}$$

Dimensions: what are n, d, dk, dv, V …?

Most confusion about attention is shape confusion, so fix the symbols first. In this report each row is one token (the lecture slides draw the transpose). Rules to keep in mind

  • $Q$ and $K$ must have the same width $d_k$ (their dot product needs matching lengths); $V$'s width $d_v$ is free, and the output has $d_v$ columns. The conventional choice is $d_k=d_v=d/h$, so that $h$ heads concatenated again have width $d$.
  • $V$ (vocabulary) never appears inside attention. It only sets the size of the embedding table and of the final output layer. Attention sees only $n$ and $d$.
  • $n$ (sequence length) appears in the number of rows of every activation, and quadratically in the score matrix $n\times n$: doubling the input length quadruples the attention cost and memory ($\approx n^2 d$ multiply-adds, $n^2$ scores per head). No learned weight has a dimension $n$, so the same weights process any length (limited in practice by what was trained, and by the position encoding).
  • $d$ (width) sets the number of parameters: one attention layer has $W_Q,W_K,W_V,W_O$ = $4d^2$ weights (when $hd_k=d$); the feed-forward net has $2\,d\,d_{ff}\approx8d^2$.
  • Worked shapes, mini model: $X$ is $6\times2$, $W_Q$ is $2\times2$, $Q$ is $6\times2$, scores $6\times6$ (36 numbers), $Z$ is $6\times2$. BERT-base-sized layer ($n=512$, $d=768$, $h=12$, $d_k=64$): $X$ is $512\times768$; per head $W_Q$ is $768\times64$, $Q$ is $512\times64$, scores $512\times512\approx262\,000$ numbers; 12 heads give 12 such score matrices.

“+ positions”: off / ON

Off: the input to attention is just the token embeddings, $X=E$. Each token's vector depends only on which word it is, not where it stands.

ON: the sinusoidal position vector of each slot (B5c) is added: $X=E+PE$. Now “teddy” in slot 3 and “teddy” in slot 5 have different vectors, so their queries and keys, and hence all the weights, change.

Why it is critical: attention scores are computed from content only, so with positions off the layer sees a set, not a sequence. Try it: shuffle the words with positions off – every token's output is unchanged (it merely moves with its word). With positions ON the outputs change after shuffling: the layer can now tell the orders apart.

“causal mask”: off / ON

Off: every token may look at every token, left and right (this is what the encoder does, e.g. to understand a source sentence).

ON: before the softmax every score with $j>i$ (a token to the right) is replaced by $-\infty$:

$$A=\mathrm{softmax}\Big(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\Big),\quad M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&j\le i\\-\infty&j>i\end{cases}$$

$e^{-\infty}=0$, so those weights are exactly 0 (upper triangle of the heatmap turns white) and each row is renormalised over the allowed part: row 1 (“a”) can only look at itself, weight 1.00; the last row still sees everyone.

Why it is critical: a text generator must predict token $i+1$ from tokens $\le i$. If training let position $i$ see the future it would just copy the answer. The mask lets one pass train all positions at once without cheating, and matches generation time, when later tokens do not yet exist. This is the “masked self-attention” of the decoder in B6.

Guided tour (2 minutes). (1) Everything off, query “bear”: which tokens get big discs? (2) Press “shuffle words”: the row for “bear” shows the same weights on the same words, just in another order, and the output vector is identical – permutation-equivariant (permuting the input permutes the output the same way; the layer has no idea of order). (3) Switch positions ON and shuffle again: outputs now differ (look at the “largest change” in the text below the plots). (4) Switch the causal mask ON: upper triangle vanishes; select “a” and see weight 1.00 on itself. (5) The green ✓ line confirms that the JavaScript equals the NumPy result for the three un-shuffled settings (plain, positions, positions+mask).

Take-away: with positions off, shuffling the words changes nothing for each token's output (the layer treats its input as a set – permutation-equivariant). That is why the Transformer needs position information (B5), and why the causal mask (B6) is a deliberate choice.

B5 · Step 5 – three details that make attention work

(a) Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

In plain words. The score of a query–key pair is a sum of $d_k$ products. The more terms you add up, the more the sum can wander away from zero, so scores get bigger in size as $d_k$ grows – for no good reason. Big scores make the softmax almost one-hot (“winner takes all”), and a one-hot softmax has almost zero gradient, so learning stalls. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ cancels exactly that growth.

The argument. Assume (as at the start of training) that the entries of $\mathbf q$ and $\mathbf k$ are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1. The score is $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k=\sum_{m=1}^{d_k}q_mk_m$.

  1. Each term has mean $\mathbb E[q_mk_m]=\mathbb E[q_m]\,\mathbb E[k_m]=0$ (independence).
  2. Each term has variance $\operatorname{Var}(q_mk_m)=\mathbb E[q_m^2k_m^2]-0=\mathbb E[q_m^2]\,\mathbb E[k_m^2]=1\cdot1=1$.
  3. The $d_k$ terms are independent, and variances of independent variables add: $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k)=d_k$. Hence the standard deviation is $\sqrt{d_k}$ (not $d_k$: the spread grows like a random walk, with the square root of the number of steps).
  4. Multiplying a variable by a constant $c$ multiplies its variance by $c^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}\!\big(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k/\sqrt{d_k}\big)=d_k/d_k=1$: the scaled scores have unit spread whatever $d_k$ is.

Why a large spread hurts. Softmax weights are $a_j\propto e^{s_j}$; a gap of 8 between scores (typical at $d_k=64$ unscaled, where the std is 8) means a factor $e^8\approx3000$, so one key takes ≈ 100 % of the weight. And the softmax derivative $\partial a_i/\partial s_j=a_i(\delta_{ij}-a_j)$ is ≈ 0 whenever the $a$'s are (nearly) one-hot, so gradients vanish. Equivalently, $\sqrt{d_k}$ is a fixed temperature. The assumption (independent unit-variance entries) holds only roughly and only at initialisation, but it explains why the fix is needed; the widget checks it empirically (std of q·k in its read-out).

Softmax saturation vs head dimension

(b) Multiple heads

Why more than one? One attention computes one softmax per token, hence one weighted average, one “view” of the sentence. But a token usually has to attend to several different things at once for different reasons: bear may need its adjective (cute), its verb (reading) and the previous token, each carrying a different kind of information. Averaging all of that with one set of weights blurs it. The fix: run $h$ independent attentions (heads) side by side, each with its own learned $W_Q^{(i)},W_K^{(i)},W_V^{(i)}$, so each can specialise in a different pattern (like several filters in a convolution).

$$\mathrm{head}_i=\mathrm{Attention}(XW_Q^{(i)},XW_K^{(i)},XW_V^{(i)}),\qquad \mathrm{MHA}(X)=\mathrm{concat}(\mathrm{head}_1,\dots,\mathrm{head}_h)\,W_O$$

Nothing forces the heads to differ: they start from different random initialisations and each is free to reduce the same loss, so gradient descent tends to push them to different jobs. In trained models some heads indeed look at the previous token, some at syntactic partners, some at rare keywords; many turn out redundant (pruning studies, e.g. Michel et al. 2019, remove many heads with little loss).

Shapes and cost. With the usual $d_k=d_v=d/h$ each head works in a smaller space, so $h$ heads cost about the same as one full-width head:

stageshapeexample: original paper, $d=512$, $h=8$
input $X$$n\times d$$n\times512$
per head: $Q^{(i)},K^{(i)},V^{(i)}$ ($W^{(i)}$ is $d\times d/h$)$n\times d/h$$n\times64$, eight times

Role of $W_O$. Concatenation just places the $h$ results side by side; $W_O$ is the learned matrix that mixes them so that information from different heads can combine in each output coordinate. Toy case: with $d=16$ and $h=2$ (the sense Transformer above) each head has $d_k=8$; the 2-D mini model of the explorer has $h=1$ (with $d=2$ a second head would have only 1 dimension). In code the $h$ heads are computed at once by reshaping $XW_Q$ from $n\times d$ to $h\times n\times d/h$.

(c) Position encodings

Attention has no sense of order, so a vector encoding the position is added (not concatenated: the width stays $d$) to each token embedding: $X=E+PE$. The original paper uses sines and cosines of different frequencies:

$$PE_{(pos,2i)}=\sin\!\Big(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\Big),\qquad PE_{(pos,2i+1)}=\cos\!\Big(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\Big)$$

Reading the formula. $pos=0,1,\dots,n-1$ is the position of the token in the sequence. The $d$ coordinates are grouped in $d/2$ pairs, indexed by $i=0,\dots,d/2-1$; pair $i$ is $(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ with angle $\theta=\omega_i\cdot pos$ and frequency $\omega_i=10000^{-2i/d}$.

  • A different clock speed per pair. For $i=0$, $\omega_0=1$: the angle advances 1 radian per position, a full cycle every $2\pi\approx6.3$ positions (the fastest “second hand”). As $i$ grows, $\omega_i$ shrinks geometrically down to $\approx1/10000$ for the last pair, whose wavelength is $2\pi\cdot10000\approx63\,000$ positions (the slowest “hour hand”). The constant 10000 sets how slow the slowest clock is, i.e. which lengths can still be told apart.
  • Like the digits of a counter. The last digit of a number changes at every step, the next one every 10 steps, and so on; together the digits identify the number uniquely. Likewise the pairs, fast to slow, jointly give each position a unique fingerprint.
  • Why a sine and a cosine? A pair is a point on a circle. Moving $k$ positions further rotates it by the fixed angle $\omega_ik$, whatever the starting position: $PE_{pos+k}=R(\omega_1k,\omega_2k,\dots)\,PE_{pos}$ with $R$ a block-diagonal rotation. So “k tokens later” is a linear function of the encoding, which makes relative offsets easy for the $W_Q,W_K$ to read off. It also means nearby positions have similar encodings (second plot below).
  • No parameters are learned and nothing depends on the sequence length: the encoding of position 5 is the same in a 10-token and a 1000-token input.
Sinusoidal position encoding

Left: the matrix $PE$ ($n$ rows = positions, $d$ columns; red/blue = value $+1$/$-1$). Right: cosine similarity between the encodings of two positions: bright diagonal = identical; similarity falls as positions get further apart (the oblique ripples are the fast frequencies), but it stays well above 0 here because the slow columns are nearly the same for all of these positions.

Trends to look for when you move the sliders.

  • Along the columns (left → right): stripes get wider. The leftmost columns alternate within a few rows (fast), the right-hand ones change slowly and for short sequences are almost constant from top to bottom (sine ≈ 0, cosine ≈ 1: the angle has barely moved; this is the grey/red column pattern on the right of the heatmap). Those slow columns carry no information at $n\le64$; they matter for long contexts.
  • Increase $n$ (more positions): rows are added at the bottom; the rows already there do not change (the code is independent of $n$). Fast columns show more oscillations, and once you go beyond the slower wavelengths the slow columns start to change as well. The similarity matrix grows a longer diagonal band; far-away positions stay different.
  • Increase $d$ (wider vectors): more columns, and the frequencies $10000^{-2i/d}$ are sampled more finely between the same two limits (1 and 1/10000): the leftmost column is always the fastest, the pattern in the leftmost columns is the same, but you get more pairs in between. More slow-to-fast “digits” means a finer, more unique fingerprint and a sharper similarity peak; at $d=4$ (only two frequencies) different positions can look alike (look at the off-diagonal ripples of the similarity plot).
  • Magnitude: every entry is in $[-1,1]$, so the encoding has fixed norm $\sqrt{d/2}$, small compared with how large embeddings can become, which is one reason implementations scale the embeddings.
Beyond the lecture The sinusoid is only one design. Learned absolute position embeddings (a trainable table of $n_{\max}\times d$; BERT, GPT-2) work equally well in-range but cannot go beyond the trained length. Relative schemes put the offset $j-i$ directly in the score (Shaw et al. 2018; the learned bias of T5). Rotary embeddings (RoPE), used in Llama-type models, rotate $\mathbf q$ and $\mathbf k$ by position-dependent angles using the very same frequency set, so that $\mathbf q_i\cdot\mathbf k_j$ depends on $j-i$ only. ALiBi subtracts a penalty proportional to distance from the scores. Long-context models mostly use RoPE variants.

B6 · Step 6 – the Transformer, assembled

The original model is built for translation: an encoder (left) turns the source sentence into context-aware embeddings; a decoder (right) produces the target sentence one token at a time. Both are stacks of $N$ identical blocks (the original paper used $N=6$).

Quiz in the lecture In the cross-attention arrow(s), which of Q, K, V comes from the decoder?
The query. The decoder asks “which words of the input matter for what I'm about to say?”; the keys and values come from the encoder, i.e. from the input sequence.

Generating. Start with <bos>; the decoder outputs a distribution over the vocabulary; take the next token, append it, feed the sequence back in, and repeat until <eos> is generated. Masked means position $i$ may only attend to positions $\le i$ – at generation time later tokens do not exist yet. In practice the mask adds $-\infty$ to forbidden scores before the softmax.

Label smoothing – a training trick from the paper

After “What a great …” many words are valid (day, lecture, book). Training with a hard one-hot target says “100 % this word, 0 % all others”. Label smoothing keeps $1-\varepsilon$ on the true word and spreads $\varepsilon$ over the others, so the model is told it may be wrong:

$$y_k=1-\varepsilon,\qquad y_{j\ne k}=\frac{\varepsilon}{V-1}$$

Step-by-step walkthrough

C Practical aspects of the idea

Choosing a tokenizer and vocabulary
  • Single language: a subword tokenizer is the usual choice – it exploits word roots with a lower OOV risk than word level.
  • Vocabulary size: tens of thousands for one language; hundreds of thousands for multilingual and code models.
  • Always reserve an <unk> slot for out-of-vocabulary tokens, and <bos>/<eos> to mark start and end of sequences.
  • Train the tokenizer on text that looks like your use case (2026): a mismatch lengthens sequences and raises cost.
  • Why sequence length matters: compute grows with the number of tokens – subword and especially character-level tokenizers pay for it.
Choosing sizes
  • Embedding / hidden size $D$ (or $d_\text{model}$): hundreds to thousands (e.g. 768). Trade-off: task complexity versus latency and cost; choose empirically and copy what others found to work.
  • In the Transformer the feed-forward hidden layer is wider than its input/output (unlike word2vec's bottleneck) to give the model degrees of freedom.
  • Number of heads $h$ and stacked blocks $N$ are hyper-parameters; the original used $N=6$.
  • Hidden size also sets the cost of everything downstream.
Training and decoding practice
  • Proxy tasks: watch the loss versus epochs and stop when it plateaus, but remember the loss is not the real goal: judge embeddings on the downstream task.
  • Metrics by task: use precision/recall/F1 (not accuracy) on imbalanced classes; per-token or per-entity-type metrics for NER; BLEU/ROUGE need reference texts which are costly, perplexity does not (the lecture previews LLM-based, reference-free evaluation for later).
  • Parallelism: unlike RNNs, all positions of a sequence are processed at once with matrix products – a perfect fit for GPUs.
  • Generation is sequential: one token per decoder pass until <eos>.
  • Label smoothing (ε≈0.1 in the original) improved BLEU on translation; it is a general technique.
  • Heads are not constrained to differ – they diverge because of what gradient descent finds useful.
  • Reading list: the course book “Super Study Guide: Transformers & LLMs” and the VIP cheat sheet (both mentioned in the lecture).
Beyond the lecture Rough sizes for the original base model ($d=512$, $d_{ff}=2048$, $h=8$): attention has $4d^2\approx1.05$ M parameters per block, the feed-forward network $2\,d\,d_{ff}\approx2.10$ M, so ≈3.2 M per block (before biases, embeddings and the cross-attention in decoder blocks). The score matrix $QK^\top$ has $n^2$ entries per head, so memory and time grow quadratically with sequence length – the real price of “every token looks at every token”.

D The conclusions

Take-aways
  1. Text must become numbers: tokenize (word / subword / character) then embed. Subword (BPE) is the sweet spot.
  2. One-hot vectors are orthogonal and carry no similarity; learned embeddings (word2vec: a proxy task like next-word or neighbours prediction) place related words close together.
  3. Static embeddings ignore context and order. RNNs/LSTMs add order, but forget over long distances (vanishing gradients) and are sequential and slow.
  4. Attention gives every token a direct link to every other: $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V$, with learned $W_Q,W_K,W_V$; multiple heads learn different relations; position encodings restore order; the mask restricts the decoder to the past.
  5. The Transformer stacks encoder blocks (self-attention + FFN) and decoder blocks (masked self-attention, cross-attention, FFN) with residuals and layer normalisation; generation runs until <eos>.
  6. Evaluate with metrics that fit the task and data: F1 on imbalanced classes; BLEU/ROUGE with references; perplexity from the model's own probabilities.

Where this leads

Lecture 2 (2025) takes the original architecture apart (position embeddings in detail, attention variants) and shows how today's models relate to it; later lectures turn it into large language models, train and align them, give them reasoning, tools and evaluation.

What the mini model does not show

  • All data is synthetic and tiny (vocabulary of 14–31, 2–16 dimensions). It illustrates mechanisms, not scale or real language.
  • The 2-D embedding space has little room: books, money and function words end up close together; the clean separation seen with larger $D$ in real models is not reproduced.
  • We built the encoder only (the decoder's cross-attention and mask are tested in NumPy, but no translation model is trained).
